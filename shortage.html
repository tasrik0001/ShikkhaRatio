The staffing question / Dataset year 2024

Every student needs a teacher with time to teach.

Time to explain a difficult idea. Time to notice who is falling behind. Time to answer one more question. Teacher availability matters because these small moments matter.

Start with the people behind the ratio

My interest in teacher workload began with visits to my mother’s classroom. Looking at district figures is a way to take that question further: how does the balance between students and teachers differ across Bangladesh?

In the 2024 secondary-school records, there are 9,063,422 students and 293,289 teachers. Dividing students by teachers gives a national ratio of 30.90 students per teacher. Across districts, the figure ranges from 20.72 in Jessore to 49.91 in Sunamganj.

What does students per teacher mean?

The ratio, labelled TSR in the explorer, compares the total students and teachers reported for the same district and sector. A higher ratio means more students for each reported teacher across those records.

It is not the size of an actual class. Teachers may work with different groups, subjects or shifts. The World Bank’s pupil-teacher ratio definition also distinguishes this measure from classroom conditions.

When does pressure become a shortage?

A school might have enough teachers overall but still need someone to teach a particular subject. Another might have vacant posts or teachers who are unavailable. Understanding those needs takes school-level information about posts, subjects and the staff actually teaching.

The district ratio helps us decide what to ask next. It does not count vacancies or tell us how many new teachers to appoint. A lower ratio is also worth understanding, rather than assuming that a district has teachers to spare.

Look closer to home

Find your district and look at its student and teacher counts together. Compare it with another district in the same sector. What would you want to ask a teacher there about the time available for lessons and individual help?

The numbers give that conversation a starting point. Listening to teachers and students can help us understand what the figures leave out.
